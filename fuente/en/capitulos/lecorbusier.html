Le Corbusier. The modern journey

Historical documentation and reconstruction

Charles-Édouard Jeanneret (1887–1965), who would adopt the name Le Corbusier from 1920, trained at the School of Art in La Chaux-de-Fonds, his home town, under Charles L’Eplattenier. Before the journey to the East he had completed an itinerant training: the 1907 journey through Italy, a stay in Vienna and work in the offices of Auguste Perret in Paris (1908–1909) and Peter Behrens in Berlin (1910–1911). After the journey he would become one of the central figures of the Modern Movement.

The sources available for reconstructing this journey are the texts of the Journey to the East, six sketchbooks (carnets), photographs and the reconstruction developed in Daza Caicedo’s doctoral thesis. Among his youthful journeys, that of 1911 offers especially explicit testimony to the importance of the experience in his way of interpreting architecture.

Le Corbusier set out in May 1911, aged 23, after his time with Behrens in Berlin, where he had begun to plan the journey. In Dresden he met up with his friend August Klipstein, an art history student, to travel East, with a long stay in Constantinople (today’s Istanbul) as the culmination and an unhurried passage through Greece. Jeanneret referred to it as his voyage utile, his “useful journey”, and presciently conceived it as an initiatory journey: he planned it already disposed to be transformed by it.

According to the GIS reconstruction, the route left Dresden at the end of May and headed down through Prague and Vienna to Bratislava and Budapest, then followed the Danube through Hungary and Serbia (Baja, Mohács, Novi Sad, Belgrade). It crossed Romania (Bucharest) and Bulgaria (Tarnovo, Gabrovo, Kazanlak, Stara Zagora) and, via Edirne, reached Constantinople, the heart of the journey. From there, after a stop in Bursa, the travellers went on to Mount Athos and Greece (Thessaloniki, Athens, Delphi), crossed to Italy via Brindisi and travelled through Paestum, Pompeii, Naples, Rome, Florence, Pisa and Genoa before returning, via Milan and Lucerne, to La Chaux-de-Fonds at the beginning of November.

Jeanneret and Klipstein spent 43 days in Constantinople, 26 % of the five and a half months (165 days) the journey lasted; it was by far the longest stay.

The route combines the stops of the classical journey —Athens, Rome, Naples— with the novelty of the eastern route: the partial descent of the Danube, the Balkans and the detour to Constantinople. The long stay in that city reflects an intention of romantic spirit, the search for an exoticism that classical journeys, such as the one Jeanneret himself had made in 1907, did not address.

Although both journeys, of 1907 and 1911, left a strong documentary trace in the architect’s archive, only the second clearly changed the young Charles-Édouard Jeanneret’s way of seeing. At the end of his account, he himself expresses disappointment with the last part of the journey, in which he passed again through the Italian cities he had visited four years earlier, and implies that the places discovered on the second —Constantinople, Athens, Delphi— had left a much deeper mark on his view of the world and of architecture:

“I am deeply affected by all these things of Italy. I had lived four months of masterly simplicity: the sea, mountains of stone with the same profile —Turkey with its mosques, its wooden houses, its cemeteries, Athos with convents closed like a prison around the single Byzantine church; Greece with the temple and the hut: the earth was bare. […] Why is our progress ugly? Why do those who still have virgin blood hasten to take the worst from us? Do we care for art? […] Shall we never again make harmony?” (own translation from the Spanish edition)1

Like the romantic travellers, Le Corbusier wrote in memoir form, in the first person, recounting what befell the two friends along the way. In his graphic record he made extensive use of photography, but he still produced numerous sketches, which show the duality of note-taking on the modern journey: detailed studies of matters that interested him, very free sketches of Constantinople’s skylines and drawings of buildings that sought not a refined record but an understanding of composition, proportion, spatial quality or setting.

The journey to the East is probably, among the case studies of this thesis, the one that has been written about most. The texts that make up the Journey to the East2 were written during the journey, in 1911, but were not published until 1966, after being revised by Le Corbusier shortly before his death in 1965. Le Corbusier also documented the journey in six carnets de voyage, small pocket sketchbooks in which he drew, held at the Fondation Le Corbusier in Paris and published in facsimile3. This documentation has made it possible to reconstruct the journey with great precision, as Ricardo Daza Caicedo does in his doctoral thesis Tras el viaje de Oriente4.

Influence on later architecture

The literature on the influence of the journey to the East on Le Corbusier is extensive, but it is Daza Caicedo’s thesis that most concretely establishes the connections between the architecture visited and the architect’s later production. In other cases, as will be seen, it is Le Corbusier himself who explicitly acknowledges in later texts the influences taken from the journey.

Although the journey crossed Eastern Europe, the Balkans, Turkey, Greece and Italy, it was to Turkey and Greece that Le Corbusier devoted most time and attention, and of which he speaks most highly in his notes and later writings.

The study of influences therefore focuses on these two destinations, with partial inclusion of southern Italy, since the north, and Tuscany in particular, had been visited on the 1907 journey5. Some of the cases analysed by Daza Caicedo are taken as a reference.

In Turkey, Le Corbusier was captivated by Istanbul, where he made the longest stay of the journey and of which he left abundant notes and drawings.

His study of Hagia Sophia stands out, of course. In it he highlights the handling of scale between exterior and interior: in contrast to a relatively compact and modest exterior, the impression of entering the great central space under the dome without a rectangular nave to serve as a transition. The idea recurs throughout the journey, in which he goes so far as to compare the experience of entering Hagia Sophia, the Pantheon of Agrippa and St Peter’s in the Vatican, arguing in favour of the first two because of the surprise of entering a contained space of such dimensions:

“The spatial impression produced by the Pantheon is instantaneous —similar to that of Hagia Sophia—: when visitors pass through the portico and cross the imposing bronze door, they receive the impact of the dome and the light entering through the oculus. In Michelangelo’s original design for St Peter’s, after the enlargement of the vestibule and the new façade by Carlo Maderno, […] In Vers une architecture, Le Corbusier would express his irritation” (own translation)6.

Inside Hagia Sophia he also highlights the play of light beneath the dome. According to Daza Caicedo, Hagia Sophia became for Le Corbusier a model that he reinterpreted in later works such as the church of Saint-Pierre in Firminy, where he sought to recreate the light and spatial sensation of entering the Byzantine church.

Continuing with the Istanbul influences, another mosque deeply impressed Le Corbusier: the Süleymaniye, which he studied closely and whose setting in the city he admired, high on one of its hills and dominating the Golden Horn. Daza Caicedo suggests that it may have influenced the design of the Immeuble Clarté in Geneva, through the stepped crowning of the building and its “entrance devices”, a common feature of Ottoman mosques that Le Corbusier also used in the Curutchet House and the monastery of La Tourette.

The monasteries of Mount Athos and La Tourette

Continuing towards Greece, on arriving at Mount Athos Jeanneret and Klipstein visited several Orthodox monasteries from which Le Corbusier would take references for the monastery of La Tourette. The relationship is not mere supposition: in one of his 1954 sketches of La Tourette, Le Corbusier drew from memory a diagram of the monastery of Simonopetra.

As a general lesson from the monasteries of Mount Athos, Le Corbusier adopted for La Tourette the idea of establishing an upper horizontal plane from which the building hangs down towards the ground. At La Tourette, this artificial horizontal plane allowed him to build while adapting to the irregular natural terrain as it suited him, which also reduced construction costs.

Continuing with the Greek influences on the same monastery, Daza Caicedo identifies several compositional features taken from the Parthenon and reinterpreted at La Tourette, such as the misalignment of one of the Parthenon’s columns with respect to the peristyle, which he compares with La Tourette’s chimney, or the position of the Erechtheion in relation to the Parthenon, which he relates to the volume projecting from the monastery’s north façade.

Beyond these formal references, Le Corbusier himself acknowledged the influence of the Acropolis on other works, such as the Unité d’habitation in Marseille, on whose roof he sought to recreate the sensation of climbing the Acropolis, a high place from which the Aegean can be seen reappearing above the surrounding mountain range:

“It is a perception similar to the one he had on the Athenian Acropolis, where he saw the Aegean reappear after having been overwhelmed by the mountain range. A similar intention will be evident in the Unité in Marseille, on whose terrace Le Corbusier enhances the role of the sea and the mountainous geography by placing a high parapet at eye level” (own translation)7.
