Case studies, discussion and impact on later architecture

Choice of case studies

The first criterion is typological representativeness. Each selected journey predominantly displays the characteristics of the type to which it is assigned, in terms of its historical context, geography, architecture of interest, motivations and forms of record. This assignment does not mean that the journeys belong exclusively to one category, since, as noted above, the different types can coexist and share certain characteristics.

The second criterion is the educational relevance of the journey. Experiences have been selected which, because of their motivations, biographical circumstances or surviving testimonies, show signs of having been particularly important in the architect’s education and development. This criterion relates to the concept of the initiatory journey developed in the theoretical framework. Its possible transformative character is not, however, taken as proven in advance, but is a question to be examined through the analysis of each case and its relationship with later architectural production.

The third criterion is the availability and quality of documentary sources. Reconstructing the journeys requires information about the itineraries, the places visited and the experiences recorded by their protagonists. To this end, both primary sources, such as sketchbooks, drawings, correspondence and photographs, and retrospective testimonies and historiographical studies are used. The nature and precision of these sources determine the scope of the analysis and the degree of certainty of the reconstructions.

Finally, the historiographical relevance of the selected architects is considered. The five cases correspond to recognised figures in the history of architecture, whose careers and works have been widely studied. This makes it easier to access documentation and literature that help contextualise the journeys and examine their possible relationship with later production. At the same time, it introduces a selection bias towards established architects, whose documentation has been preserved and studied more often, as Ceserani and her team warn1.

In accordance with these criteria, the following journeys are selected:

  • John Soane, classical journey (1778–1780)

    • Chosen because it amply meets all four criteria and, in particular, because Soane stands out among his contemporaries for his later historiographical relevance.

  • Charles Garnier, romantic journey (1868)

    • Garnier’s journey lacks a confirmed educational relevance in his later architecture. This shortcoming is, however, offset by its typological representativeness and, above all, by the quality of its documentary sources, the best of the five cases.

  • Le Corbusier, modern journey (1911)

    • Like Soane’s, this journey amply meets all four selection criteria.

  • Jørn Utzon, vernacular journey (1958)

    • This journey is chosen above all for its educational relevance; it also has a fair number of documentary sources and previous research, though not at the level of availability of the previous three.

  • Tadao Ando, contemporary journey (1965)

    • Although Ando’s journey is the weakest in terms of documentary availability, it is highly representative, amply meets the other three criteria and has the added interest of being one of the few researchable journeys made by an Eastern architect towards the West.

Documentary availability is uneven across the five cases, which affects the precision of the reconstructions and the possibilities for analysis. This limitation is especially significant in Ando’s case, whose itinerary can only be partially reconstructed from the sources consulted. His selection is maintained because of its interest for exploring the broadened characterisation of the contemporary journey proposed in this thesis, with the uncertainties explicitly noted and documented data distinguished from estimates.

Reconstructing the journeys

Organising the information

To analyse and compare the case studies, a database has been created that organises the information with a twofold aim, in line with specific objectives SO3 and SO4. On the one hand, it serves as an ordered repository of information on each journey: a kind of contemporary travel notebook, no longer physical but digital and georeferenced, which other researchers can consult. On the other, it is an analytical tool: with the information ordered in space and time, it is easier to compare the journeys and draw conclusions.

A Geographic Information System (GIS) has therefore been chosen, which makes it possible to reconstruct the architects’ journeys and to associate the selected information with each place. The selection reflects what was learned in the state of the art: from Ceserani’s model comes the spatio-temporal reconstruction of the itineraries; from Chiu’s, the need to link each place to the architecture visited and to the documents that record it; and from the classification of types, the features that make it possible to compare the journeys with one another (geography, duration and mode of record). It is worth distinguishing between two kinds of information: data (places, dates and sequence), entered as attributes of each record, and documents (drawings, sketchbook pages, letters and photographs), which are not transcribed but linked to the places where they were produced.

Information gathered and criteria

The information is organised as georeferenced points, each representing a stay in a place on a specific or estimated date. Each point includes the following attributes:

Fid Native QGIS field
Place Name of the city, area or country
Arrival date Date of arrival at the place
Departure date Date of departure from the place
Sequence Field for ordering the sequence of places visited, so the route can be ordered
Document Links graphic documentation or archival correspondence to the place where it was made

Each attribute answers a specific question, derived from the state of the art and the classification of types:

Place. Makes it possible to trace the geographical extent of the journey, one of the criteria that distinguish the types, and to relate each stay to the architecture visited there.

Arrival and departure dates. Make it possible to calculate the duration of each stay and, with it, the distribution of the journey’s time: what percentage of the total was devoted to each place or region. This information helps identify prolonged stays and compare how the journeys were organised in time. Duration is not, however, regarded on its own as a measure of architectural interest or of the intensity of learning, since it may reflect circumstances of various kinds. This relationship will be analysed in each case.

Sequence. Orders the stays and makes it possible to reconstruct the route, distinguish prolonged stays from excursions and detect returns to the same place.

Documents. Links to each place, in georeferenced form, the graphic or written documentation produced there when it is available in digitised form in the architects’ archives, so that the GIS also acts as an index to the architect’s archive: it shows which sketchbook entries, drawings or photographs correspond to each location and thus makes it possible to analyse how each journey was recorded. This applies especially to Le Corbusier, Utzon and Soane, since they have institutions that hold and digitise their archives.

The information available is not equally precise in every case. While some journeys can be reconstructed in detail from contemporary documentation, others depend partly on retrospective testimonies or indirect references. When the sources do not make it possible to establish a location, date or duration with certainty, documented information is distinguished from reconstruction estimates and hypotheses. These limitations are explicitly noted and taken into account when interpreting the results.

Representing and analysing the journeys

The reconstruction in the GIS precedes and underpins the description of each case study: the durations, percentages and sequences cited in the following sections derive from it. From the GIS, two graphic representations are produced for each journey. The first is an itinerary map, showing its geographical extent. The second is a chronological-geographical axis, in which time runs horizontally and each place occupies a row: each stay is represented as a bar whose length is its duration, so that the sequence of the route, the long stays, the excursions and the returns can be read at a glance. This axis is accompanied by a sheet with the basic data of the journey (duration, the traveller’s age and countries visited) and by an axis of the architect’s life, which places the journey at its point in their career. Taken together, the GIS also make it possible to compare the geographical spread of the journeys over the course of their historical evolution.
