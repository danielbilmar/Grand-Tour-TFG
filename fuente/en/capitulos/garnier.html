Charles Garnier. The romantic journey

Historical documentation and reconstruction

Charles Garnier (1825–1898) trained at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris and in 1848 won the Grand Prix de Rome1, which allowed him to reside as a pensionnaire at the Villa Medici. During those years he completed his training with a classical journey in every respect: he travelled through Italy and journeyed to Greece —where he studied the Temple of Aphaia on Aegina— and to Constantinople. His own biography thus shows how the classical journey institutionalised by the academies was still current in the mid-nineteenth century. In 1861 he won the competition for the new Paris Opéra, the building that would define his career, and he later designed, among other buildings, the Monte Carlo Casino.

The source available for reconstructing his journey is the manuscript of his travel diary, written in verse and republished in facsimile, which contains a detailed itinerary and numerous drawings.

In May 1868, aged 42, Garnier was already an established architect. The Opéra had been under construction for almost seven years and its building had been complicated from the outset by water in the subsoil; later, the Franco-Prussian War (1870–1871) would interrupt it again. In this context, the journey to Spain was a way of temporarily escaping the pressure of a project that was dragging on.

Garnier’s age and the point he had reached in his career raise doubts about the initiatory character of this journey. It was not an educational journey, since Garnier had already completed his training in Rome, nor does it show a priori the threshold condition that defines the initiatory journey. Its inclusion is due above all to its representativeness as a romantic journey, and its initiatory character is put forward here as a hypothesis: that of a journey that initiated the architect into architectures —Islamic and Spanish Baroque— foreign to his academic training, at a time when important decisions about the Opéra’s decoration were still open. This hypothesis is discussed in the section on influence.

Garnier travelled with his wife, Louise, and two friends, Gustave Boulanger and Ambroise Baudry. The journey lasted 29 days and described a large circuit around the peninsula. He left Paris at the beginning of May and, after passing through Bordeaux and Bayonne, entered Spain at San Sebastián; he crossed Vitoria, Burgos and Valladolid and reached Madrid, where he made his longest stay and from where he visited El Escorial. From there he headed south via Toledo and Alcázar de San Juan towards Andalusia: Córdoba, Seville, Cádiz, Tarifa, Algeciras, Málaga, Antequera, Granada and Jaén. The return, again via Alcázar de San Juan, ran through the Levant —Elda, Alicante, Valencia, Castellón and Barcelona— and, via Girona and Perpignan, back into France, reaching Paris on 1 June. Andalusia, with around ten days, was the region to which he devoted most time.

The account of this journey, Viaje a España2, whose manuscript survives and has been republished in facsimile, includes a table in which Garnier recorded the itinerary in extreme detail: he noted the departure and arrival times of the trains and even the towns he passed through without stopping. This table is the basis of the GIS reconstruction.

The journey was possible, to a large extent, thanks to the dense railway network built in Spain after the General Railways Act of 1855, which increased the length of the national network tenfold3.

To understand the choice of destinations, one must bear in mind the growing interest Spain aroused among the nineteenth-century European bourgeoisie. Works such as Rossini’s The Barber of Seville (1816) or Prosper Mérimée’s novella Carmen (1845), which would later inspire Bizet’s opera (1875), had built a romantic image of the country. Southern Spain in particular attracted the Romantics’ historicist interest in an art and architecture that were exotic to European taste, the product of the peninsula’s Islamic heritage:

“In the nineteenth century, like the French Romantics and other northerners, the British took the road to Spain, drawn by its picturesque landscapes and above all by the Islamic monuments of Andalusia. The Iberian Peninsula thus satisfied their longing to know a near and accessible Orient” (own translation)4

Garnier’s itinerary had already become popular among travellers from other disciplines, such as the Briton Richard Ford, who travelled through and drew the peninsula between 1830 and 1833, or Garnier’s compatriot Alexandre Dumas, who recounted in From Paris to Cadiz5 the journey he made in 1846 as chronicler to the French court.

Ford’s drawings show the same interest in scenes, types and customs that characterises the romantic journey.

As for the record, Garnier’s diary brings together the characteristic features of the romantic traveller. It includes first-person verses describing the sensations of the journey, and his drawings depict people, dress and picturesque places, as well as, of course, architecture. The architectural drawings differ clearly from those of the classical journey: the vast majority show buildings integrated into their surroundings or into the landscape. Architecture is no longer treated as an object to be studied in meticulous detail; the aim is also to represent its harmony and its relationship with its setting.

Influence on later architecture

The influence of the journey to Spain on Garnier’s later production has not been confirmed by the documentation the architect left over the course of his life. Nor has the literature research for this thesis found studies that retrospectively analyse that influence.

One might therefore think that the journey had no impact as such on his architecture and was rather a getaway to escape temporarily from the pressure of the Opéra works.

However, a review of his later work makes it possible to point to some places where Islamic and even Spanish Baroque architecture might have served as inspiration.

A first hypothesis is that part of the Neo-Baroque interior decoration of the Paris Opéra, still to be resolved when Garnier made the journey, took some reference from what he saw in Spain. This is, however, a hypothesis that would need to be tested against specific examples of Spanish Baroque theatres and spaces.

A second hypothesis is that the more Orientalist Monte Carlo Casino took ornamental motifs from the architecture seen in southern Spain. This hypothesis faces the difficulty, however, that Garnier had already visited Constantinople during his time as a pensionnaire, in 1853, and may have encountered there architecture shaped by influences flowing back and forth between France and the Ottoman Empire, such as the Dolmabahçe Palace, then at an advanced stage of construction. The similarity of the more elaborate ornamentation in both cases makes this second route seem more plausible than the Spanish one. In any case, the absence of documentary evidence rules out asserting a direct influence, which reinforces this journey’s character as a borderline case within the definition of the initiatory journey.
