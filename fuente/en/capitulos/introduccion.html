Introduction

Travel was part of an architect’s education long before schools of architecture existed. Between the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the Grand Tour institutionalised among Europe’s elites a long educational journey to Italy that served as a rite of passage into adult life; for architects, that journey also became an almost obligatory stage of their professional training. Although the institution disappeared as such, the conviction that architecture must be known in person has survived to this day in study trips, scholarships, research stays and the mobility programmes of schools around the world.

This thesis examines that continuity through what it calls the initiatory journey: a deliberately sought journey that marks a threshold in an architect’s career —a before and after in their way of seeing— and whose lessons can be traced in their later work. The concept, defined in detail in the general context, makes it possible to compare journeys that differ greatly in form and period without limiting them to those undertaken in youth or at the end of one’s studies.

To organise this evolution, the thesis takes as its starting point the classification of architectural travel proposed by Gonçalves Diez and adapts it into five types of journey —classical, romantic, modern, vernacular and contemporary—, each linked to the historical and artistic context in which it emerged, though not confined to it. For each type a representative case study is analysed: John Soane’s journey through Italy (1778–1780), Charles Garnier’s through Spain (1868), Le Corbusier’s journey to the East (1911), Jørn Utzon’s journey through China (1958) and Tadao Ando’s journey to Europe (1965).

Methodologically, the thesis adopts a historiographical approach supported by the reconstruction of the itineraries in a Geographic Information System (GIS), following the model of Stanford University’s Grand Tour Project, and compares what was visited with each architect’s later production. David Kolb’s experiential learning theory provides a framework for understanding why the journey —and not only its built outcome— deserves to be reconstructed in full.

Methodology

Phase 1: Literature review and classification

Search for sources and review of the literature and archives, arranging the bibliography on the Grand Tour and architectural travel chronologically. In this phase the phenomenon is analysed in general terms, which makes it possible to become familiar with the practice and its characteristics and to establish an initial classification of journey types, inherited from the literature (Gonçalves Diez) and adapted in this thesis. The criteria for choosing the case studies also derive from it.

Outcome: bibliographic database; chronological axis with the types of journey and their characteristics.

Tools: reference manager (Zotero); databases and repositories (idUS, ResearchGate, Scopus); archives (Fondation Le Corbusier, Sir John Soane’s Museum, The Utzon Archives).

Phase 2: Gathering and organising information on the case studies

Selection of one architect and one journey representative of each type, according to the criteria established in the previous phase.

Gathering information on each journey: places, dates of each stay, sequence of the route and archival material (drawings, sketchbooks, correspondence, photographs).

Reconstruction of the journeys in a GIS that incorporates the information gathered.

Outcome: a combined GIS organised by route, with the stays linked to sketchbook entries, photographs or drawings.

Tools: QGIS; databases; online catalogues and digital collections (Sir John Soane’s Museum, Fondation Le Corbusier, The Utzon Archives, Google Arts & Culture) and the archives that hold the architects’ drawings.

Phase 3: Analysis of the case studies

Historical contextualisation of each case study and analysis of the route, the motivations for the journey, the idea driving it and the architect’s personal and professional circumstances at the time.

Outcome: contextualisation of each journey and a table of its characteristics (duration, the traveller’s age, number of countries visited, etc.).

Tools: GIS; original graphic work (itinerary maps and chronological-geographical axes for each journey); comparative tables.

Phase 4: Impact on later architecture

Literature search and analysis of each architect’s work.

Analysis of the work built after the journey in comparison with the buildings visited or drawn during it.

Outcome: conclusions on the impact of each journey.

Tools: comparative matrices, timelines and graphic comparison between buildings visited and buildings designed.

Phase 5: Conclusions

Comparative synthesis of the five case studies, and contrast of the results with the classification of journey types and with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle.

Outcome: general conclusions, limitations of the thesis and future lines of research.

Tools: comparative table of the cases and combined GIS.
