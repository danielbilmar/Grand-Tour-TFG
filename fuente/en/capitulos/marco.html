Theoretical framework

A brief state of the art

Gathering information for this thesis required drawing on a wide range of sources, with very different approaches to the Grand Tour and architectural travel.

The scattered nature of the case studies rules out any specific body of literature covering the practice of the Grand Tour as it evolved over the centuries. There are relevant publications focused on particular periods or travellers, but no overall view of that evolution; this is the gap that the present thesis seeks to help fill.

The study of the Grand Tour and, more broadly, of travel as part of an architect’s education has been approached from different historiographical and methodological perspectives. Some studies have focused on reconstructing travellers’ itineraries and experiences from documentary sources; others have analysed the influence that the places and buildings visited may have had on their later production. At the same time, various authors have studied the transformation of the historical Grand Tour and its continuation, under new forms and interests, in contemporary architectural travel.

This state of the art is organised around these lines of research, paying particular attention to studies that make it possible, on the one hand, to understand the historical evolution of architectural travel and, on the other, to establish methods for reconstructing and analysing the itineraries undertaken and their possible influence on architectural practice.

One of the most relevant studies, both historiographically and methodologically, is the work carried out within The Grand Tour Project at Stanford University, led by Professor Giovanna Ceserani. Among its outputs, the most relevant publication for this thesis is the article “British Travelers in Eighteenth-Century Italy: The Grand Tour and the Profession of Architecture”1.

The project team reconstructs the journeys in GIS format by digitising the Dictionary of British and Irish Travellers in Italy, 1701–1800, compiled by John Ingamells from the Brinsley Ford archive, which in turn gathers correspondence, diaries and other documents from the travellers themselves. In this way it places thousands of travellers, among them a good number of architects, in space and time.

This way of working is the main inspiration for the methodology of this thesis, whose central tool is the reconstruction of the journeys in GIS format to facilitate their representation and analysis.

Historiographically, the article is also useful for characterising the classical journey and, in particular, John Soane’s journey, to which it devotes special attention.

In the article “China Receives Utzon: The Role of Jørn Utzon’s 1958 Study Trip to China in His Architectural Maturity”2, Chen-Yu Chiu uses interviews and archival work to reconstruct Utzon’s 1958 journey through China. On the basis of that reconstruction, he establishes connections between the architecture Utzon visited and the architecture he later designed, using the itinerary as a basis for identifying them.

This study is taken as a reference for its method of relating the architecture visited on a particular journey to the architecture later designed by the traveller himself, formulating hypotheses about possible inspirations and references through the comparison of compositions, ornamental elements and construction elements.

Finally, in the article “Roma–Passaic–Las Vegas: del Grand Tour al viaje arquitectónico contemporáneo”3, Smara Gonçalves Diez analyses the interests that have driven architectural travel throughout history, with particular attention to contemporary travel, whose founding moment she places in the trip made by Robert Venturi, Denise Scott Brown and Steven Izenour to the Las Vegas strip in 1968.

This article is especially influential for the present thesis because it sketches an initial historical division of journey types and sets out their main characteristics. It also lends theoretical weight to contemporary practice, by justifying the interest of new generations of architects in visiting cities not only for their monumental value but also out of anthropological curiosity, an issue this thesis also addresses.

These studies give rise to the three pillars of the thesis: from Ceserani, the method for reconstructing itineraries in space and time; from Chiu, the method for contrasting what was visited with later work; and from Gonçalves Diez, the initial classification of journey types. To these is added Homiński’s study of study tours, discussed in the next section, which supports relating the time spent in each place to its later impact.

The concept of the Grand Tour

Origin of the idea

The term Grand Tour was coined by the English cleric Richard Lassels in The Voyage of Italy (1670)4, where he described the journeys that young members of the British aristocracy and upper middle class made to Italy to see classical and Renaissance art at first hand.

The practice enjoyed great social prestige as a guarantee of the quality of these young men’s education. Besides direct contact with classical and Renaissance art, the journey was expected to build a network of contacts among young people of high society that would favour future business, commissions and collaborations.

Prominent figures from every discipline made a Grand Tour during these centuries; among the best known are Adam Smith, Goethe, Mary Shelley and the Mozart family.

Making this journey granted young men a licence of freedom and marked their entry into adult life. It was also a sign of status that was practically essential for success in their later professional life.

It is worth qualifying, however, who had access to this journey. The canonical figure of the grand tourist was a young man from the privileged classes, a gender marker that Spanish tends to conceal when speaking generically of jóvenes (“young people”). Women’s mobility and education were subject to strong social and economic constraints, and their access to the architectural profession was practically non-existent throughout this period. This did not prevent women from travelling (Ceserani’s team itself includes them among the participants in the Grand Tour), and recent studies5 6 have asked precisely how their journeys differed from those of men. Although this thesis does not address the question in depth, the fact that all its case studies are men should also be read in the light of these constraints.

The architects’ Grand Tour

In its early days, the Grand Tour was not an architectural journey. What travellers went to see was, above all, classical Antiquity (its ruins, its sculptures, the places known through Greek and Latin texts) and Renaissance art, together with the courts, languages and customs of the continent. Architecture was part of this programme as one more element of the classical legacy, contemplated by amateurs and collectors rather than studied by professionals.

Over the course of the eighteenth century this changed and the journey crystallised into a specifically architectural matter. Architects began to travel as professionals: they measured and drew the ruins, trained in the Italian academies and competed in their competitions, and acted as guides and tutors to other travellers, with whom they forged relationships that would shape their careers on their return. The academies institutionalised the journey through scholarships for residence in Rome, such as those of the pensionnaires of the Académie de France à Rome, founded in 1666, the Real Academia de España en Roma, founded in 1873, or the American Academy in Rome, founded in 1894.

The discoveries of Herculaneum (1738) and Pompeii (1748) and the rediscovery of the Greek temples of Paestum reinforced the shift: the journey ceased to be merely a pilgrimage to the art of the past and became a direct source of models, proportions and ornamental repertoires for neoclassical architecture. As Ceserani’s team puts it:

“Not everyone who went on the Grand Tour became a successful architect, but almost every successful British architect of the eighteenth century had been on the Grand Tour. It was a crucial educational experience”7.

The drift of the practice

Although the Grand Tour in its original definition did not survive as such, travel as a tool in an architect’s education largely retains the spirit Lassels described more than three and a half centuries ago. The disappearance of the institutionalised journey did not mean the disappearance of the educational journey, but its transformation: the destinations changed, as did the means of transport, the ways of recording what was seen and, above all, what architects went in search of.

After the eighteenth century no institutionalised figure with the force of the classical Grand Tour can be identified, but the journeys made by architects of every period, up to the present day, can be considered practices descended from it. For this reason, the thesis analyses that evolution through the changing references of architects’ journeys, and assesses their importance by setting what was visited against each architect’s later production.

The importance of experiential learning

Beyond the destinations and the models it provided, the Grand Tour was above all an educational experience based on the direct observation of architecture. In contrast to knowledge acquired from books, treatises or engravings, the journey made it possible to walk through spaces, analyse buildings in their context and test prior ideas against reality. That passage from what is lived to what is learned is precisely the subject of Experiential Learning Theory, formulated in 1984 by David A. Kolb, who holds a doctorate in social psychology from Harvard University8. According to Kolb, experience does not become knowledge immediately, but through a four-phase cycle:

  1. Concrete experience: the acquisition of unmediated knowledge through the senses.

  2. Reflective observation: a detached review of what has been experienced, which makes it possible to set what was expected against the experience itself.

  3. Abstract conceptualisation: reflection gives way to the modification or generation of an idea rooted in empirical experience.

  4. Active experimentation: the idea is tested by interacting with the real world again, which restarts the cycle.

Although Kolb developed his theory for modern teaching contexts, some researchers have transferred its structure to study tours. This is the case of Homiński, who in his article “Study tours in teaching architecture and urbanism: improving the itinerary”9 interprets the architectural visit as experience in Kolb’s sense. His study analysed the work of 40 students who took part in three study tours of European cities and compared the time spent at each building with how often it was later chosen for in-depth study. For several buildings, visits of more than an hour were associated with a greater presence in that work. His results therefore point to the relevance of direct experience as a starting point, though without verifying the complete cycle.

Homiński’s own study qualifies this relationship. Large urban projects, despite receiving long visits, hardly appeared in the students’ in-depth studies, which Homiński tentatively attributes to the difficulty of perceiving interventions that exceed the scale of a single building and to the greater time needed to explore them. Conversely, several buildings with short visits were frequently chosen, which indicates that other factors come into play, such as the accessibility of information, fashion, students’ interests, the influence of peers or prior training. From Kolb’s perspective, this suggests that exposure time alone is not enough for concrete experience to feed the later phases of the cycle: the object must also be graspable by the visitor.

Kolb’s experiential learning theory offers an interpretive framework from which to understand the educational potential of the Grand Tour. Direct contact with architecture during the journey can be related to concrete experience; drawings, notes and correspondence make it possible to identify processes of observation and reflection; and the development of one’s own architectural criteria can be linked to abstract conceptualisation. Finally, the application of this knowledge in later projects and buildings parallels active experimentation. This correspondence does not imply that every traveller necessarily completed all four phases, but it makes it possible to recognise different dimensions of the learning associated with the journey. From this perspective, the Grand Tour can be understood not only as an experience of getting to know and observing architecture, but also as a process of reflection on and transformation of architectural thinking, whose possible consequences can be traced in the later production of its protagonists.

Concrete experience The journey itself
Reflective observation Sketchbooks and correspondence
Abstract conceptualisation Development of a language of one’s own from the references
Active experimentation Later production (a consequence of the journey)

The initiatory journey

Experiential learning helps explain how direct contact with architecture can contribute to an architect’s education. However, not all journeys carry the same weight in an architect’s career. While some broaden knowledge or add new references, others can profoundly transform the way architecture is observed, interpreted and designed. It is in this second case that the concept of the initiatory journey becomes particularly relevant.

Over the course of a career, an architect may make journeys of different kinds: study or educational journeys, organised to complete formal training; professional journeys, linked to buildings, competitions or commissions; and leisure journeys, in which architecture plays a secondary role. These categories are not mutually exclusive and may overlap in a single journey. The initiatory character, however, is not one more category within this classification, but a condition that can arise in any of them when the journey represents a turning point in the architect’s career.

The term derives from the original sense of the Grand Tour as a rite of passage, but here it is not necessarily identified with the first journey after one’s studies or with a youthful journey. An initiatory journey is understood as one that meets three conditions: it is longed for and deliberately sought in order to acquire new references, often foreign to the architect’s previous training; it marks a threshold in their career, so that one can speak of a before and after in their way of looking at architecture; and its lessons can be traced in their later work.

The transformation of the gaze is therefore a consequence of the initiatory journey, but it is not enough on its own to define it: what characterises it is its condition as a threshold and the intention with which it is undertaken. For the same reason, what matters is not the traveller’s age but the point in their career at which the journey takes place. This definition guides the selection of the case studies.
