John Soane. The classical journey

Historical documentation and reconstruction

John Soane (1753–1837) was born in Goring-on-Thames, the son of a bricklayer. He trained in the offices of George Dance the Younger and Henry Holland and in the Royal Academy Schools, where in 1776 he won the gold medal for architecture1. The prize earned him the travelling studentship granted by the King through the Royal Academy, which funded his stay in Italy2. After his return he would build one of the most distinguished careers of British Neoclassicism: he was architect to the Bank of England from 1788 and professor of architecture at the Royal Academy from 1806, and he turned his house in Lincoln’s Inn Fields into the museum that now holds his archive.

The sources available for reconstructing his journey are the correspondence, travel notebooks and drawings held at Sir John Soane’s Museum and accessible through its online catalogue, which allow the route to be reconstructed in great detail. There are also Du Prey’s monograph and Ceserani’s study.

In March 1778, aged 24 and having just completed his training, Soane left London for Italy. His journey followed the model of the academies’ scholars in Rome, such as the pensionnaires of the Académie de France à Rome: young artists and architects awarded scholarships to complete their training in direct contact with Antiquity. This model is important because it turned the journey into a regulated stage of an architect’s education. Like those scholars, Soane conceived the journey almost as a residence in Rome, from which he made excursions to other cities in Italy, Sicily and Malta. It was, therefore, an educational journey in the most literal sense, made at the end of his studies and at the height of Neoclassicism.

The route can be summarised simply. Soane arrived in Rome in May 1778 and stayed there until December. In early 1779 he moved south and settled in Naples, from where he visited Pompeii, Salerno and Paestum; he returned briefly to Rome in spring and, again from Naples, travelled to Sicily and Malta in May and June. In July he returned to Naples and Rome. In August and September 1779 he made a rapid foray north —Ancona, Bologna, Parma, Milan, Brescia, Verona, Mantua, Padua and Venice— and returned via Florence to Rome, where he spent the winter. Finally, in April 1780 he set off back to England, travelling at a somewhat more leisurely pace through, among others, Siena, Florence, Ferrara, Padua, Vicenza, Verona, Mantua, Parma, Milan, Pavia, Genoa and Como.

The GIS data make it possible to specify this distribution. The journey lasted 753 days, of which Soane spent 478 in Rome, divided into four periods, amounting to 63 % of the total. Rome and the south —Naples, Campania, Sicily and Malta— together account for 82 % of the time. Among the other cities he visited from Rome, Naples stands out: he returned there as many as five times, with stays of at least five days on each occasion.

This concentration in the south suggests a particular interest in settlements of Greco-Roman origin and a priority of classical over Renaissance art, as confirmed by his deliberate visits to Paestum, on two occasions, and to Pompeii.

The journeys north, by contrast, were rapid and account for a smaller share of the time. The first had the character of a reconnaissance tour, notable for the stop in Padua and Venice; the second was already the return journey, which Soane cut short, according to Ceserani and her team, to take advantage of the professional opportunities on offer in England. Even so, his passage through the Veneto was not irrelevant: as will be seen, Palladio’s imprint is visible in his residential architecture.

The sketches in his notebooks are what one would expect of a classical journey: a strong emphasis on representing reality, faithfully copying proportions and ornament, with plans and analytical notes on what he visited. Only very rarely do his drawings show the building’s surroundings or the landscape; this study of architecture as an object in itself is another indicator of the Enlightenment spirit of the journey. His notebooks also record, with almost accountant-like thoroughness, the stages and expenses of the road, as shown by the entry for his arrival in Milan:

“From Genoa to Milan / Arrived Sunday 12 o’clock - 21st / Sundries on the Road - 0.3.0 / Carriage - 55 - 2.1.3 / At Milan / See the great Hospital in the Town / & the Cemeteries of the same &c near the / walls of the town”3

Note: the date under which the archive records this entry appears to be wrong, since it places it in 1779, and 21 May of that year was not a Sunday.

Influence on later architecture

Soane himself described the importance the journey had had for his career:

“I was sent to Italy to pursue my studies […] This was the most fortunate event of my life, for it was the means by which I formed those connections to which I owe all the advantages I have since enjoyed”4.

These words suggest that the importance of the journey lay largely in the network of contacts Soane established in Italy. As Du Prey explains in his monograph on the architect5, Soane formed at least eight important relationships there with people who later gave him commissions or recommended him to new clients. Foremost among them was Frederick Hervey, Bishop of Derry, his main patron during his stay in Italy.

The journey also left a clear formal influence on his architecture. Soane is regarded as one of the great architects of Neoclassicism, a movement in which ornamental motifs, compositions and orders are transferred directly from classical architecture.

His more institutional architecture offers clear examples of this transfer of what he saw in Italy into what he later built.

This is the case of the Bank of England, where Soane was architect between 1788 —eight years after his return from Italy— and 1833. In the celebrated Tivoli Corner he reinterpreted the Temple of Vesta at Tivoli, near Rome, adapting it to resolve the corner of the building.

In the same building, the now-lost Rotunda of the Bank of England sought a play of light similar to that of the Pantheon of Agrippa, which Soane had drawn meticulously in Rome. Later, inspired by the engravings Piranesi had given him during his stay in Rome, Soane commissioned Joseph Michael Gandy to produce a series of views showing the Bank as if it were a Roman ruin. The Rotunda itself was immortalised in a plate produced in Soane’s office and was eventually demolished during Herbert Baker’s rebuilding of the Bank between 1921 and 1939.

The influence of the journey can also be seen in smaller works. In the Barn à la Paestum, a barn in Solihull, Soane drew on the proportions and order of the Temple of Neptune at Paestum, of which he kept notes in his travel notebook, demonstrating his interest in the primitive Doric he had encountered in southern Italy.

In his residential architecture, Soane showed a clear preference for Palladian villas: his designs bear a striking resemblance to the works of the late-Renaissance master, both in the composition of the façades and in the layout of the plans. This preference is consistent with his longer stops, on the journeys north, in the Venice–Vicenza–Padua triangle, Andrea Palladio’s birthplace and the area of his greatest activity.
