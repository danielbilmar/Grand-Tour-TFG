Historical documentation and reconstruction

Tadao Ando (Osaka, 1941) is a self-taught architect: his family’s financial situation prevented him from going to university, and he trained through reading, work and, above all, travel. Before setting off for Europe, he travelled around Japan whenever he could to see every kind of architecture:

“Whenever I had time, I travelled around Japan looking at all kinds of architecture, from old buildings in the Kansai region to the latest modern architecture. Naturally, that led me to think about the rest of the world, and in 1965, a year after international travel was generally permitted, I spent the money I had saved from part-time jobs and went to Europe alone” (own translation)1

The sources available for reconstructing this journey are retrospective testimonies collected in interviews and in his autobiography, together with a small number of published photographs. The graphic material from the journey included in the catalogue of the exhibition Tadao Ando: Endeavors (2017) could not be consulted.

In 1965, aged 23, Ando made his first journey abroad, funded with money saved from temporary jobs and made possible by the liberalisation of tourist travel abroad approved by the Japanese government in April 1964. His main motivation, as he has recounted in various interviews, was a second-hand book of Le Corbusier’s drawings bought at the Tengyu Shoten bookshop in Osaka. The Swiss master’s work captivated him to the point that he set off with the idea of meeting him in person at his Paris studio, a dream he never fulfilled: Le Corbusier died at Roquebrune-Cap-Martin on 27 August 1965, a few weeks before Ando arrived in Paris.

“From then on, I went over the drawings every night. Little by little, my desire to ‘experience Le Corbusier’s spaces at first hand’ and to ‘meet him in person’ grew stronger; I think the direct trigger for my first journey around the world was this desire to ‘meet Le Corbusier’” (own translation).2

There is no exact documentation that would allow the chronology of the journey to be established. It lasted about seven months and included, besides Europe, the return through Africa and Asia.

Ando himself describes the first part of the itinerary in Conversations with Students:

“So in 1965 I decided, with great enthusiasm, to go to Europe to meet him [Le Corbusier]. At the end of April I sailed from Yokohama to Nakhodka, in what was then the Soviet Union. On arrival I took the Trans-Siberian Railway and travelled to Moscow, passed through Leningrad (today St Petersburg) and, via Finland, finally reached Paris in September” (rendered from the Spanish translation)3

At the beginning of the same book he briefly describes the start of his return to Japan, skirting Africa and crossing India. Some Japanese sources place the rest of the return by ship via the Philippines, although no direct source has been found in which Ando confirms this last stop4.

“More than thirty years ago, I made the journey home from my first trip to Europe. After waiting several weeks in Marseille, I boarded a ship back to Japan, making stops at the Ivory Coast, Cape Town, and Madagascar before eventually crossing the Indian Ocean to arrive in Mumbai. There, as if drawn by some mysterious force, I boarded a train to the sacred land of Varanasi in northern India”5.

During his stay in Europe, before sailing from Marseille for Africa, Ando mainly visited the architecture of his idol, Le Corbusier —the Villa Savoye, the monastery of La Tourette, the Unité d’habitation in Marseille—, as well as classics of architectural history such as the Parthenon, the Pantheon in Rome or the Sagrada Família in Barcelona.

After this tour of Europe from September 1965, Ando is estimated to have sailed from Marseille for Africa between October and November, skirted the continent and reached India at Bombay.

Ando’s journey is the hardest of the case studies to reconstruct in space and time: the accessible archival documents published by his office amount to two photographs, one at a stop on the Trans-Siberian and another at the Parthenon in Athens. What is known of the journey’s record is therefore mainly these photographs and the architect’s later testimonies. There is literature that could complete this information —in particular, the catalogue of the exhibition Tadao Ando: Endeavors (National Art Center, Tokyo, 2017), which reproduces the notes from his travel notebook6—, but it could not be consulted for this research.

Influence on later architecture

The influence of the journey on Ando’s later architecture cannot be understood without the idea that prompted it in 1965: the desire to meet Le Corbusier.

Although this stage of his life is poorly documented in publications accessible from the West, Ando himself has written and recounted in numerous interviews how the places he visited in 1965 —most of them works by Le Corbusier— influenced his later works.

In an article by the Mori Art Museum summarising the ideas Ando shared in a lecture at the institution, the architect speaks of the strong influence of seeing Le Corbusier’s work in Europe.

In the life he witnessed at the Unité d’habitation in Marseille, during the weeks he spent there waiting for the ship home, he locates the origin of the idea of “margin space”, which he has developed throughout his practice: the importance of the spaces of encounter between people that architecture has the power to create. This idea is a constant in his career, as can be seen in works such as the Rokko Housing complex, where intermediate and circulation spaces are given special care to encourage these “encounters”.

Speaking of the Azuma House, Ando points out that he took the Villa Savoye as a direct reference, structuring the living space around an inner courtyard that articulates the routes through the house and blurs the boundaries between inside and outside7.

In the same lecture he spoke of another of his architectural obsessions: sculpting light and creating spaces in which light is the protagonist. In this field, he acknowledges having taken Le Corbusier’s chapel at Ronchamp as a reference for the Church of the Light in Ibaraki (Osaka) and for the Chichu Art Museum on Naoshima.

Finally, he acknowledges the relationship between his 4×4 House in Kobe and Le Corbusier’s Cabanon, the retreat where the master spent his summers in his last years: both are very small houses, bound to their position facing the sea, which seek expression through space rather than size:

“Architecture is not a matter of physical size, but of space”8

Ando’s case shows that, in the contemporary journey, influences are not necessarily explicit in terms of composition and form. What predominates is an influence based on perception —a more phenomenological matter—, in which inspiration comes more from the lived experience of space, light and use than from formal quotation. This supports the refinement of the contemporary journey proposed in this thesis: a journey that combines pilgrimage to the masterpieces of modern architecture with a phenomenological experience of space.
